SESSION 18: THE FALSE LIGHT
A possible DM rehearsal. This is preparation, not a prediction of player decisions or a report of events. Listen privately. The successful route follows Scott's intended arc: manage the attack, recover the mill object, and head north with it and the plate. Alternative choices remain playable. New object and rendezvous details are proposed.

ONE. THE MAP BECOMES A TOWN

Imagine the last light of the afternoon resting on Greenest. It touches roof tiles, the rough edges of the road, and the flour dust on the mill's upper window. Nothing about the town suggests that it understands the plan being made around it. A woman gathers washing before the damp comes up from the river. A child carries a bowl toward the sanctuary. Somewhere a door is being repaired, and the repeated knock of a hammer sounds ordinary enough to be reassuring. These small tasks are what the party is protecting. The attack should feel important because people have lives here, not because the sky needs another spectacle.

The council scene is over. You do not need to run it again. The papers have been read, the map taken, the route puzzle solved. The party earned the right to put markers on that map. Now those markers have feet, names, equipment and questions. Both work crews have gone to the mill with Floyd GoldSeeker and Brother Kai. Two guard teams have followed Pat-Benatar toward the ridge. The third waits on the west path with Throk. Severed Whisper has taken the north road alone, with all the confidence Scott described and the possible tree concealment Erik also mentioned. Begin by asking which posture he actually chooses. That simple question gives the player his character back before the danger starts.

Picture Escobert at the edge of the mill yard. He has already given the forces the town can spare. He does not need to become an army commander who narrates the whole battle for them. He checks a knot, asks a runner to repeat a destination, and makes sure someone remembers where the casualty cart will wait. His competence gives the scene texture. His questions make the players' decisions concrete. Nighthill's concern is equally ordinary: he needs to know which doors remain guarded and who will bring him reliable news. The baseline town watch has not vanished just because the party reassigned three teams.

Give the opening recap in a voice that moves from memory into the immediate present. They rescued Doss at the Chalk Cut. They followed the tools. They found a camp whose wagons were dressed to look like a threat and whose picks were meant for something more valuable. They took the clues and map, watched a dragon fly north, kept the plate, and brought evidence home. The recap should acknowledge their actions. Do not tell them they negotiated brilliantly, saved everyone, or understood every mystery. They have done enough to stand here with a plan, and the next part belongs to tonight.

The first drum sounds beyond the fields. Leave a little room after it. The second is farther away or merely softened by the road, and then the two begin to establish a rhythm. At the mill, a worker raises their head. On the west path, a guard stops adjusting a strap. On the ridge, a hand rests closer to a lantern shutter. On the north road, Severed Whisper hears the wheels. You now have four scenes connected by one sound. You can move between them without pretending that everyone can hear everybody else's conversation.

TWO. PAT-BENATAR AND THE LIGHT

The ridge is a place of cooling stone and grass that brushes the guards' legs. Below it, Greenest is beginning to show its lamps. One patch of road is visible, then another, and the low eastern route can be picked out by its darker line along the water. This is the place the signal paper described. The players solved that geography last session; reward it with immediate recognition. Pat-Benatar does not have to roll to discover that she came to the right hill. The question is what she does with the people and equipment already there.

The observers are two hired people doing a job. One keeps watching the town, trying to see whether its defenders move north when the drums begin. The other waits beside the shuttered lantern. They are not already sending the long flash. The order was conditional, and the condition matters. The party has spread its reassignable forces among the ridge, west path and mill. That is different from a large visible rush toward Wheel. Until the observers see a movement that fits their instructions, they hold. This gives Pat-Benatar a real opportunity created by the party's understanding of the papers.

Imagine her choosing to put one guard team along the retreat path. The other stays close enough to support her approach. It is a sensible plan. Do not answer sensible preparation by secretly adding four more observers behind a rock. She has two teams against two watchers because that is what the table chose and what the enemy paper said. A quiet approach may still be uncertain. A watcher may turn at the wrong moment. But the numerical advantage and blocked retreat should remain useful. The exciting question can be whether she gets the lantern intact and keeps the alarm from carrying downhill, rather than whether she survives an invented boss fight.

She may order surrender. She may fire first. She may send a guard to speak while she approaches from another angle. Let the scene reflect the actual choice. If violence happens, use the familiar hired-raider record and keep it short. The observers want escape or a chance to warn someone. If cornered, they can surrender. If the party chooses to kill them, resolve that choice through the actual attacks and situation. Do not narrate their deaths because the prior plan used the words kill the people on the lantern. Intention is not an already resolved action.

The lantern matters as equipment. A guard can knock it over while securing the position. Pat-Benatar can catch it, order someone to keep it covered, or decide the light is less important than an escaping observer. Tell her what is visible before asking for a roll. A shuttered brass lamp is a tool with a handle, a flame, and a direction. It is not an artifact that reads her intentions. If it is captured intact, give her the satisfying physical moment of resting her own hand where the observer's hand was a minute before. The town and the low route are now in her view.

This is where her main bet becomes specific. Ask what pattern she sends and what she wants the recipients to believe. Repeated flashes were proposed. No light was also mentioned at the end of the night. One long flash is the enemy's written signal. These choices do different things. Repeated flashes can confuse an instruction-bound crew and make it seek clarification. Darkness can hold the crew in place. One long flash can release it if the rest of the observed situation looks right. Do not convert a panic-flash joke into a universal secret code that makes all enemies run wherever she wants.

Picture the successful disruption version first. She chooses a series of short irregular flashes, enough to be obvious, none long enough to meet the release order. Down on the drainage approach, someone stops and looks uphill. A foreman asks a companion to count what they saw. There is a disagreement, then a decision to wait for a runner. That is success. The party bought time and broke coordination. It may look less theatrical than an entire enemy column charging into the wrong valley, but it is a direct consequence of a smart choice. Give the player clear evidence that her action has changed the night.

Now picture the lure version. She wants Spade to come toward the prepared mill. The party needs a convincing visible movement north, or a message that explains why the light contradicts what the workers see. A bluff can be tested against the recipients' actual knowledge. Pat-Benatar can coordinate through runners, use captured orders, or have a prisoner repeat a familiar instruction. Let those preparations matter. If it works, Spade moves along the real east route. If it does not, a scout investigates. Either result produces a scene the party can act on; neither needs a random penalty that erases their strategy.

THREE. SEVERED WHISPER ON THE ROAD

The wagons make noise before they make shapes. A rim knocks against a stone. Harness creaks. The drums become clearer, and then the first wagon comes around the bend, sacks piled high and shields displayed along its sides. The second follows at an interval that gives the occupants room to look impressive. Their load is straw. Their purpose is visibility. The party knows that now, and Severed Whisper can watch the performance with the confidence of somebody who has read its script. He is still alone. Confidence gives the scene flavor, not extra hit points.

If he begins hidden in the tree, describe branches, a patch of open road, and the angle from which he can see the wagon leader's face. He can count the occupants and notice that nobody is carrying a siege ladder. If he begins standing in the road, let the wagons notice him. The leader asks whom he speaks for. That gives him a chance to bluff, threaten, delay or negotiate. Do not decide that the convoy attacks a lone tabaxi on sight. Its orders are to arrive visibly, make noise, and await further instructions before entering. Severed Whisper is a complication in a demonstration.

He may tell them that Greenest is ready. He may point toward the town and imply guards they cannot see. He may claim the signal has changed. The credibility comes from what he says and what the wagon crew can verify. A bold statement can make them hesitate; an obvious contradiction can make them suspicious. Tell Erik what the leader looks at while deciding. The ridge, the townward road, the sacks, the shield line. Those glances give him a way to improve the story or choose a new tactic. One die roll should not settle every possible conversation with the convoy.

In one possible successful version, he refuses to give them the response they wanted. He stands where a whole group of defenders was supposed to gather and talks as if the noise is merely irritating. The drums keep beating, but the leader has nothing to point to as proof that the town has taken the bait. Then the unexpected flashes appear on the ridge. The leader looks up. Someone on the second wagon stops drumming for a beat and is told to continue. Severed Whisper can see the lie becoming work. The convoy's confidence starts to thin because the rest of its plan is no longer behaving predictably.

He might pursue a departing runner. That is a useful choice with a cost: leaving the wagons unwatched. He might damage a wheel or cut a drum skin. That changes the visible demonstration but can also provoke a local fight. He might offer a way out to hired people who never intended to storm a town. That can end his front without bloodshed. Prepare these possibilities, then let the player surprise you. The road scene should reward his style and his route-reading strengths without demanding that he solve four enemies in hand-to-hand combat by himself.

The convoy can leave papers, but only where that makes sense. A docket tied beneath a seat can be found if they gain access to the wagon. A captured leader can answer about the next transfer point. A runner can carry an instruction. Do not place the next adventure's entire secret on the road merely because the scene is ending. The useful clue is ordinary: a northern fork, three pale stones, a shelf where hand-carried pieces are received. The people hauling tools can know that route without knowing why a dragon wants the cargo.

FOUR. THROK'S QUIETER FRONT

The west path is quieter than the other positions. That does not make it useless. It runs past an orchard or low bank, with town lights visible through the branches. Throk has the third guard team, people who are looking to him for an order that turns waiting into a purpose. At the beginning, ask what he intends to watch or protect. Scott said the reason for this position was unclear. Do not retroactively invent a brilliant secret plan on his behalf. Give Mike a simple chance to make the choice now, with the information his character has.

He can protect runner movement, keep a possible retreat open, patrol toward the mill, or observe anyone trying to circle the town. His team can do one primary thing well. If he sends it somewhere else, its old post is no longer covered. The point is not to trap him in a logistics puzzle. It is to make his authority real enough that his decisions affect people. The ancestral theme in his story has always been protection and what comes after the blow. A guard team is a chance to express that through action without requiring a ghostly speech every time he takes charge.

The simplest prepared contact is a messenger. The person comes on foot, stops at the drums, and looks toward the ridge. A folded packet is held beneath a coat. This is ordinary information passing through the battlefield, exactly the kind of communication the enemy instructions allowed. The west path is not secretly the east drainage route, and this traveler is not all eight Spade workers compressed into one encounter. Let Throk notice the difference. No loaded picks, no heavy braces, no wagon. The person has news, questions, or a request for clarification.

Throk can stop the messenger with his team in plain view. He can let the person pass and follow. He can ask what the flashes mean. He can send a guard to escort the person to Escobert. If the messenger tries to flee, a short contest or clear blocked route may settle it. The encounter need not become a fight. The useful answer is almost embarrassingly straightforward: they were told to wait for one long flash after defenders moved north. If the light is wrong, they need someone to confirm it. Hearing that answer tells the party that its interference is working.

Throk may choose restraint with a prisoner. Brother Kai's earlier execution of the bounty hunter remains part of the group's history, but it does not require another argument tonight. Offer the circumstance; let the characters decide whether it brings tension. A frightened courier who knows a route is useful alive. A guard who sees Throk handle that person fairly may trust him more. Those are local human reactions, not a level-up reward or proof that every ancestral spirit approves. His character's choices are strong enough without an automatic supernatural verdict.

Picture the favorable version of this front: Throk has kept his people together, stopped a message from repairing the enemy's confusion, and sent reliable news toward the mill. His team is still fresh because he did not rush it into the wrong fight. When the others later ask what happened on the west path, he has a clear answer. He can also account for every guard who followed him. That is a contribution to the operation. It may not produce a dramatic pile of defeated enemies, but it helps explain why the party's larger plan can hold.

FIVE. BELOW THE MILL, ABOVE THE RISK

At the mill, the air carries damp wood and flour. The upper building is familiar. The party has already saved someone here. That matters emotionally. Floyd GoldSeeker should recognize the place where a person was trapped and the practical changes made since then. Do not reset the rescue or invent a new enormous machine he somehow missed. The old arch is still below, with its socket, its damaged channel, and the strange seam along which water climbs. The world has not changed simply because the next session needs a reveal. The next step is beside the arch, through the masonry the Spade note described.

Both crews are present. They have hands for hauling timber, passing tools and moving spoil, but they still need a safe plan. Floyd GoldSeeker can see load lines and understand why a careless breach could pull the newer mill's weight into the old structure. Give him this knowledge freely. The challenge is the choice of method, the time he takes, and the people he protects. Brother Kai has several meaningful roles. He can stand at the entrance and watch for the low-route approach. He can help pass timber into the right position. The successful signal version is quieter. Spade never receives a valid release, or its scout finds a guarded, braced entrance and reports that the diversion failed. SIX. THE THING THEY WANTED

The last block comes away after the dangerous work has been supported. Use the sound of stone grinding against grit rather than a burst of triumphant magic. Cool air slips through the gap. There is a small recess beyond, old enough that the mill feels like a newer layer placed above it. The arch itself remains closed. The party has made side access, not repaired the lock or discovered a cavern full of giant machinery. That distinction preserves what they saw in Session 16 and what the enemy orders promised in Session 17.

For this rehearsal, imagine the proposed pressure witness. It is about the length of a forearm, smoky crystal held within three old bronze ribs. It rests in a shaped cradle. One amber thread hangs still inside it. The casing bears repeated geometric marks that look related to the Observatory and the mill channels. There are no glowing words that summarize the campaign. There is no voice naming the villain, no dragon egg demanding a parental relationship, and no imprisoned soul announcing that it was Floyd GoldSeeker's missing miner. The discovery can be strange without consuming every unresolved mystery at once.

Let the players look before they touch. Floyd GoldSeeker can see that the cradle supports the instrument above the stone. Three visible clips hold it in place. Brother Kai can keep the opening safe while someone moves a lantern. Severed Whisper can check the exit or the shape of the recess. Pat-Benatar can compare the marks with the geometric family she knows. Throk can choose to stand between the fragile work and the entrance. The group is back together or connected by a clear route now. Give everybody one useful contribution instead of letting a single character's skill check swallow the whole reveal.

The object is portable, roughly ten pounds in this prepared design. A sling or two steady hands can support it. The release should feel like careful work rewarded. Inspect, support, withdraw the clips, lift. A competent description can do it. If someone rushes, describe a scrape or a tilted rib before damage happens. Give them a chance to stop. The goal is not to gate the requested next adventure behind a single die that can destroy its central object. If a player deliberately smashes it, that is a different choice and should remain consequential. Carelessness with a warning and intentional destruction are not the same scene.

Imagine the small quiet after it leaves the cradle. The mill does not explode. The sanctuary patients do not suddenly die. The object was a removable measuring component, not the sole support of all life in Greenest. The braces still matter. The damaged channel still needs its own repair. The party should be able to carry the new piece away without feeling that every action in the world has been secretly attached to it. Their responsibility is tangible: protect the object, keep the opening stable, and tell the town what remains unfinished.

If they bring the plate near, use one brief observation. A second amber line appears inside the crystal, slightly offset from the first, then fades. This gives them something to discuss. The two pieces are related. They do not yet know the full function, the meaning of the difference, or why a dragon would pay attention. Resist the temptation to answer every question with a magical projection. The old baseline can become useful information later, but tonight one visible relationship is enough to make the journey north feel earned.

In the proposed background, the pressure witness retains a limited record of how this local vault behaved before its recent disturbance. Lennithon wants the old reading and the plate's interface so he can compare it with something he has noticed farther north. That is a practical independent motive. He is not receiving orders from Frulam, and Frulam need not understand all the value of the thing she wants to possess. Keep that distinction in your own mind even if the players do not learn it tonight. A world of overlapping interests is more useful than a single faction that knows and commands everything.

Floyd GoldSeeker's old words, you were not the only one buried, dig, may be remembered by Andy. Let that happen without forcing a new divine message. He has chosen to dig with care and has brought something hidden into daylight. That action can matter to him before you decide what being spoke at Glittervein or the Observatory. If he asks whether it feels like the same light, describe only what he can observe: color, warmth, pattern, memory. Do not use a prepared reveal to settle a character's deepest question without the player's participation.

The item needs packing. A folded blanket, timber frame, or cart sling gives the party an ordinary problem to solve after the strange moment. That helps the object feel real. Count who holds it, who holds the plate, and what happens if they must run. Let the players name it if they want. Pressure witness is your working label, not a word they have automatically read on a bronze plaque. The handout should describe appearance and observed reaction. It should not print the hidden function or the dragon's motive as if the party has already proven them.

SEVEN. GREENEST LETS THEM GO

Before departure, give the town a short aftermath that reflects the actual operation. If the plan held cleanly, the quiet after the drums feels like relief, and doors open wider. If it held at a cost, there are injuries, missing equipment, and guards who need a meal before another watch. If one front remains uncertain, Escobert asks for a clear report and assigns a sensible remaining watch. Do not declare a perfect victory because the next scene is northbound. The town's condition is part of what the players chose to do, and the departure should acknowledge it.

Nighthill's thanks can be plain. He has seen evidence and now has reports. He can offer food, water, blankets and a safe place for those who rest. A coin reward or formal title is a separate decision, not something you need to invent for the plan to feel satisfying. Escobert is interested in the mill supports. He asks what the crews should keep in place and where nobody should put a load. Floyd GoldSeeker can explain. Brother Kai can confirm which people still need watching. The party has become useful to Greenest through actions, and that is stronger than an automatic appointment to command the town forever.

Doss remains in care. A sentence is enough unless the players want more. A caregiver says the leg is being treated and asks whether the travelers have news of Mara. Do not promise a miraculous cure. Picture the dawn version. Workers stand aside as the object is carried out in its padding. The mill is still a mill. Someone has to turn it, repair it and feed people after the adventurers go. EIGHT. THE ROAD ENDS AT A VOICE

Keep the northern journey simple enough that it feels like movement rather than a new session of delay. The road beyond the camp has ordinary marks: wheel ruts, rubbed cloth on a branch, packed earth where somebody shifted a load. At the fork, three pale stones have been placed too evenly to be accidental. The cart road continues one way. A narrower foot track climbs toward dark rock. Show these details before asking what the party does. Their route evidence should match enough of the ground that they can make a confident decision without a chain of mandatory navigation checks.

Offer one useful travel choice. The main road is clear and fast but easier to watch. A quieter foot route is slower and makes the wrapped crystal awkward to carry. Let Severed Whisper read the route, Floyd GoldSeeker choose how the burden is protected, and the others decide where they walk. If someone wants to scout alone, ask how they signal back and how far they go. Do not automatically ambush the separated scout because the previous session already split the party. Mara can appear if the party sought her or if the route reaches the transfer stop where she plausibly waits. She does not need to arrive as a surprise exposition device. The split shelf should feel different from the camp. No clatter of tools, no cooking line, no clerks arguing over counts. Wind passes through a gap in basalt and changes pitch. Let them decide how to present it.

At first the presence may look like the dark ridge under the overhang. Then part of it separates from the background. A breath shifts the wind. Two eyes turn toward the travelers. The players have spent two sessions learning that orders and observations matter. Their next powerful contact should live in the same world of evidence.

Imagine a short first exchange rather than a complete lore lecture. The party asks what the crystal is. He calls it a record, perhaps a witness, and asks whether they found it still seated in its cradle. He asks whether the broken channel was repaired. He wants to inspect the two pieces. That does not mean the party must surrender them. Pat-Benatar can ask for terms. Floyd GoldSeeker can ask what inspection would do. Brother Kai can ask whom the knowledge will protect. Throk can ask what happens after the exchange. Severed Whisper can ask which routes the dragon has been watching. The dragon has his own concern farther north and his own reason to want a baseline. Frulam's network wanted possession and leverage. Their interests overlap without making them one mind.

The favorable story ends with Greenest behind them and a question ahead. The town is not erased from the campaign because the party leaves; it remembers what they did and continues with the repairs they made possible. The two pieces travel north under chosen custody. The dragon is a person with interests, not an answer machine. The players have moved from finding somebody else's delivery order to deciding what they will carry and what they will ask. That is the shape to hold while you run the session. The exact road through it belongs to the table.
