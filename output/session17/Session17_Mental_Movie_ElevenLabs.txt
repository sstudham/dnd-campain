PRIVATE DM REHEARSAL — REVISED SEPTEMBER 29, 2026

What follows is one hypothetical route through Session 17. It is not actual play and does not prescribe player dialogue, feelings, choices, successful checks, NPC promises, rests or outcomes. Other branches remain valid. At the table stop wherever players choose differently.

Before the movie, hold the causal chain in mind. The original mill team tried both to reach the contents behind the sealed arch and to recover the intact plate. Damage defeated the opening attempt. They extracted the portable plate, but the party intercepted them. Now the raiders intend a physical breach beside the arch while the plate’s northward transfer continues separately. Frulam directs the raiders; Lennithon independently wants both prizes. Those names and his reasons are not opening player knowledge. The PCs have only their actual discoveries and the delivery instruction. Nothing in this rehearsal adds a large machine that Floyd failed to notice.

Imagine one possible morning.

The last sound from the cavern was water.

It had travelled upward through a groove in the stone, shining in the amber pulse from the little plate, until it reached the broken channel. There it trembled. For a moment the whole ancient wall seemed to be waiting for something. Then the light faded, and the water fell.

Floyd GoldSeeker had watched it happen more than once. He understood broken structures. A beam could carry weight only as far as its footing. A channel could carry something only as far as its next connection. Whatever these channels had been built to do, wishing at the gap would not make it whole.

Now that wall was behind them. The plate was with Throk, wrapped against the road dust. Greenest lay behind them too, its injured people waking to another uncertain day. The party walked north beneath a sky turning slowly from iron to pearl.

Their instructions had said before dawn.

Sev glanced at the brightening eastern hills and decided that a dispute about the meaning of before could probably be made to last several profitable minutes.

Pat said nothing. She was looking at the tracks. A cart had passed this way. A horse had gone north more recently, cutting across the wheel marks where the road softened. Whoever had arranged this meeting had already begun doing something about its failure.

The chalk walls rose on either side. Overnight moisture darkened their bases. Small white stones lay in the road, fresh enough to show sharp edges untouched by wheels. Then came a shout, followed by the scrape of timber and a sound from somebody trying very hard not to scream.

Floyd lengthened his stride.

Around the bend, a mule stood with its ears flat. The cart behind it leaned on uneven ground. A hired guard had wedged a length of wood beneath a slab of chalk and was putting all his weight onto the end. Beside him, a woman in travel-worn clothes was saying stop. She had apparently been saying it for some time.

A young man's hand protruded from beneath the broken timber. His face was almost the color of the chalk.

The woman looked up. Her gaze moved across the strangers. For this imagined version, Throk chose to show one corner of the plate; its brass line caught the light. If he keeps it concealed at the table, she must ask about the delivery instead.

"You're late."

Brother Kai looked from her to the trapped man.

"And you're having a difficult morning."

Her jaw tightened. "If you've brought what I think you've brought, we can both stop having one."

"Not with him pulling there," Floyd said.

The guard froze, still bent over his lever. He had the raw, furious expression of a man who had been trying to solve fear with effort. Floyd knelt without asking whose side anybody was on. He set two fingers against the cracked timber and watched the sand leaking beside it.

"Hold exactly where you are. Don't lift. Don't let go."

The young man swallowed. "I can feel my foot."

"Good," Floyd said. "Keep telling me about it."

He looked toward the cart. A chock, spare timber, rope. Enough. They did not need a miracle or another elaborate rig. They needed the upper piece to stop moving before the lower piece could be shifted.

Pat caught the mule's bridle and spoke softly until it stopped backing into its harness. Throk set the wrapped plate where he could keep it in sight and took the timber Floyd pointed out. Sev watched the road while passing down the rope. Kai crouched beside the guard and took part of the load without another word.

The woman began to object when Floyd told them to unload one side of the cart.

Pat looked at her. "Which is harder to replace?"

The woman looked at the pinned runner, then pulled the first sack down herself.

After that, the work went quickly. A brace held. Loose chalk came away in Floyd's hands. The trapped man drew one sharp breath when they eased his leg free, then another, less frightened than the first. They carried him clear of the bank and sat him against the unloaded sacks.

No one cheered. The guard rested his forehead against his own forearm. The mule attempted to eat the end of a rope.

"Doss," the young man said when Floyd asked his name. "Doss Fen."

"Then Doss Fen is staying out from under hills for a while."

Across the cart, the woman watched Throk reclaim the plate.

"Mara Venn," she said. "You could have left him. I noticed. Now tell me whether we're doing business."

Pat brushed chalk from her sleeves. "We might. We need to know who we're doing it with."

Mara gave a small, tired smile. "The person holding the money."

"That's usually the least interesting answer."

The conversation settled into the space the rescue had made. Mara still wanted the plate. The party still wanted information. But there was a man breathing beside the cart who might not have been breathing comfortably without their help, and that fact sat between every accusation they could have made.

Mara asked who had told them not to turn the channels.

Sev produced the work order with the air of someone offended by unnecessary questions. "The same person who seems to think dawn waits for contractors."

Mara read the paper. Pat watched her eyes rather than the writing. Recognition. Irritation. Worry when she reached the last line.

"Someone already went north," Pat said.

Mara looked toward the road before she could stop herself.

"A messenger. The deadline passed. Then the bank came down."

"So your employer knows the delivery was late," Throk said. "That doesn't mean they know who has it."

Mara did not answer. It was a useful silence.

She offered forty gold pieces for the plate intact. Pat asked to see the terms. Mara hesitated, then opened her satchel and unfolded the payment slip on the cart rail.

Forty gold on confirmed receipt. Plate intact. No channel test. Two initials. F. M. Transfer north. Hold for L.

Sev read it twice. He was interested in the money, but more interested in how many hands had been arranged between the payment and the person who ultimately wanted the object.

“Who is F. M.?” Pat asked.

“The initials on the authorization,” Mara said. “I collect at this cut and pass the delivery north. The next receiver handles the next leg.”

“And L.?”

“Someone farther along than me.”

This did not identify the employer. It showed a chain with more than one hand in it. Then Doss pointed toward the tool bundle under the cart.

“Those need to be back at the camp by midday. Another job at the mill tonight.”

Floyd looked up. “The mill?”

Mara unfolded a second paper. The first crew had failed to open the old arch. New work at Greenest would begin at dusk. The intact plate’s transfer continued separately. Approach and signal orders came from the survey desk at the northern camp.

The two papers described two jobs. The party did not yet know the complete plan, but they had a reason to investigate it. Someone intended to return to Greenest soon, and the place issuing the orders was within reach.

“What are they doing there tonight?” Kai asked.

“I move the delivery,” Mara said. “The survey desk tells the crews where to go.”

They could return immediately with that warning. They could watch the camp, question its workers, send a scout or try to get the instructions themselves. In this imagined version, they chose to learn enough to give Greenest a useful warning before dusk. That choice, not an expected infiltration scene, took them north.

Floyd finished securing the bandage. He did not tell Doss that everything would be fine. He told him which movements to avoid and which signs would mean he needed a healer. Doss listened with an attention he had not given the promises of his employers.

The bargain they eventually reached was narrow. Mara would introduce them as contractors asking about a disputed account, without declaring possession of the plate. In exchange, they offered Doss safe care and Mara a way out of the immediate trouble. Throk concealed the plate inside his pack before they approached camp. Nobody pretended forty gold had changed hands. This was a possible agreement they had to earn, not automatic loyalty from Mara.

Mara was helping herself as much as them. She could find someone able to resolve her account while keeping Doss safe. Her own future choices remained uncertain. The party could enter the camp under a reason somebody there would recognize.

They began moving before the chalk walls had lost their morning chill.

Floyd walked beside the cart for a while. Doss asked whether he had always known how to work with stone.

"Long enough to know it doesn't care who you work for," Floyd said.

Behind them, the rescue site disappeared around the bend. Ahead, a low thread of smoke rose beyond a fold in the hills. More tracks joined the road. Wagon wheels, boots, hooves. The work order in Pat's hand had looked like a single errand. The road was beginning to show them the size of the business.

They stopped above the shallow valley long enough to look.

The camp had the disorder of a place assembled quickly by people who expected it to function anyway. Canvas roofs leaned at different angles. Ropes crossed muddy lanes. A cook stirred a blackened pot while somebody argued about a missing bucket. Armed people moved among laborers carrying tools. The sound of an axe came steadily from the stores yard.

There were ways in besides the entrance. Sev saw them almost immediately: the western laundry line, gaps between canvas screens, a path that skirted the workers' shelters. He also saw the raised watch post near the southern approach. Two guards. A bell. A clear view of anybody making a spectacle at the gate.

He pointed with one claw, very slightly.

Pat followed the gesture. "I see it."

Floyd's attention went to the laborers. A limping man balancing a bundle. A woman washing dust from her arms. People eating before their next shift. He had worked beside people like these. He had also watched employers spend them carelessly.

Mara waited by the cart. "They'll ask why there are five of you."

"Difficult load," Sev said.

“If you show them what you have, they will want it,” Mara said.

“Then we are asking about paperwork,” Sev answered.

For the first time, Mara almost laughed.

At the delivery entrance, a clerk sat beneath a piece of canvas that provided shade to his ledger and none to his head. He looked at Mara, at Doss's leg, and finally at the five strangers.

"What happened?"

"The cut came down," Mara said. "They got him out. The delivery terms need settling."

The clerk pushed the ledger toward her. "Everything needs settling."

It was Pat who kept the exchange from becoming an interrogation. She asked where disputed deliveries were recorded. She asked who could verify the receipt. She sounded as though she had spent half her life being sent between people who insisted that somebody else was responsible.

That was a language the clerk understood.

He directed them toward the wagon yard and told them not to wander. Mara took Doss toward the workers' quarters. The guard went with them, carrying the unloaded sacks. Throk kept the plate concealed inside his pack. The clerk knew only their cover and the account question. If the plate were exposed or Mara reported it, the clerk would call for a receiver and guards, ask for handover and watch their movements. Low pay would not make him ignore an object the camp wanted. The party would have to negotiate, withdraw, hide it or confront a recovery attempt.

The wagon yard smelled of straw and axle grease. Two vehicles stood ready near the road. Their sacks rose in the suggestive shape of provisions, but a split seam showed pale stalks instead of grain. Nearby, six shields leaned against a wheel. Two drums lay under a cover.

Pat slowed.

At a distance, the wagons would look supplied. With shields on the sides and drums beating, perhaps they would look like the beginning of a much larger movement.

She did not yet know why that mattered.

A yard clerk wanted to know whether they were drivers. Sev said they had been sent to settle an assignment and gestured toward the gate. It was a truthful description arranged to invite a useful misunderstanding.

The clerk handed him a dispatch tally and told him that if anybody had changed the load again, they could come and change it themselves.

Sev read the heading. Wheel.

Two wagons. Straw rather than grain. Six shields. Two drums. No ladders, ram, or provisions for an occupation. Arrive where the town could see them at dusk. Make themselves heard. Await further orders before entering.

He handed the paper to Pat. She read it while pretending to check the wagons against the list.

"Dusk," she said, softly enough that only her companions heard.

The town had a deadline now. A real one, printed in somebody else's preparations.

Kai looked at the shields. "We could break the axles."

"And then we'd know exactly as much as we know now," Pat said. "With everyone looking for us."

He considered that. "Later, then."

She did not argue with later.

Floyd saw Mara helping Doss beneath a workers' awning. He went to check that the runner had a place to sit with his leg supported. A nearby laborer watched him arrange the sacks. Their hands were ink-stained around the nails, a surveyor's stain, with chalk still caught in the cuffs.

"You know your loads," the stranger said.

"You know your measurements," Floyd answered.

The stranger glanced away.

It was not much of a conversation until Floyd asked what they had been surveying. Then it became no conversation at all. The worker bent over a cup and said they only measured what they were told to measure.

Floyd sat down nearby. He did not block the way out.

"I used to tell myself that about digging."

The worker studied him. "Used to?"

Floyd rubbed a thumb over the old marks on his tool handle. For an instant, the camp noise faded beneath another remembered sound: picks striking rock in air that had become too thin. He did not recount Glittervein. He asked whether the worker had people in Greenest.

"People I know."

"Then we have something in common."

Pat came over carrying the tally. She let the worker see the paper without shoving it into their face. The stranger looked toward the eastern stores, then toward the records tent.

"I measured the low route," they said. "They said it was access for repairs."

"Is it?" Floyd asked.

"It could be. That's what makes it difficult."

The survey assistant had kept a copy of their instructions. Spade. Eight pairs of boots. Picks, pry bars, rope and timber braces. Follow the water on foot. Reach the mill entrance, then the shallow space beneath. Brace the work, breach masonry beside the sealed arch and retrieve what lay beyond. Do not await the plate or waste time on the damaged lock. Keep the mill and channels standing. Move only on one long shuttered flash; otherwise hold.

Floyd read the paper carefully. The outside route brought the crew to the mill. Their tools were for the next step: physically making a way around the sealed arch. They were returning for whatever the first team had failed to reach. The plate remained valuable, but keeping it away from this crew would not prevent their work. Nothing required a machine Floyd had overlooked; the plan used masonry he had already examined.

Pat asked if the worker wanted to leave.

"Can you get me out?"

She looked toward the gate rather than making an easy promise. "If you're willing to come with us, we'll make a plan."

The worker nodded. That was enough for now.

Across the camp, Sev had found the records tent. Through its open front he could see a broad table, rolls of parchment, weights, rulers, and a map whose careful linework made him forget the money on Mara's slip for several seconds.

A records keeper looked up from a stool.

"Delivery question," Sev said.

"Of course it is."

"We were told to verify the route."

The keeper pointed at the table. "Look. Don't rearrange anything."

Permission was a wonderful thing. Sev liked it almost as much as the absence of witnesses.

Throk stood in the doorway with the plate concealed inside his pack. Kai stayed outside, apparently studying the camp but actually watching the watch post. Pat joined Sev at the map. Floyd followed with the survey assistant, who suddenly found reasons to keep their hands occupied with a loose measuring cord.

Greenest spread across the parchment. A main road came down from the northern camp through the chalk cut, crossed the river by the broad bridge, and reached the town's northern approach. To the east, a narrow drainage track worked through rough ground toward a foot crossing by the mill. A ridge stood above both. Westward, orchard paths threaded between trees and a narrow footbridge.

Sev leaned closer. Small measurements appeared beside the routes. Clearances. Grades. Lines of sight. Somebody had walked this ground with a purpose. Wheels could pass here. Feet could pass there. The eastern height could see things the lower ground could not.

Three symbols repeated in the notes: a wheel, a spade, a lantern.

He began making a copy on a sheet the keeper allowed him to use. His questions were practical and deliberately dull. Was this crossing measured at low water? Did the orchard bridge take wheels? Was that ridge sightline from the crest or the lower shelf?

The keeper answered two questions and told him to ask the surveyor about the third. Floyd and the assistant bent over the map. For a little while, they looked exactly like people doing work they had every right to do.

Then a guard appeared at the tent entrance.

"You were told the wagon yard."

The keeper looked up. Pat felt the room tighten.

She laid the dispatch tally beside the survey note. "The load and the route don't answer the same question. We're trying to find the person who can."

The guard looked at the papers without understanding them. That made him more suspicious, not less. He said he would stay until they were finished.

Kai stepped aside to let him in. Nothing more. His restraint left Pat room to keep talking.

Outside, a tether rope snapped taut.

The mule began to scream.

Heads turned before anyone knew why. A moving darkness crossed the southern tents, climbed the records canvas, and swallowed the light on the table. The ground seemed to gather itself beneath a pressure too large to hear properly.

For one suspended instant, the sky belonged to a dragon.

Its wings stretched beyond the edges of the tent opening. Sunlight hid its details. Air rolled down over the camp, stirring ash and flattening loose canvas. The guard went to the doorway. The keeper rose. Somebody near the cookfire dropped a ladle that struck the pot with an absurdly small sound.

Pat looked up, her hand at the place where she carried her scale. She wanted recognition. A color. A sign that would join this shadow to the distant light beyond Stormwreck. The sky gave her none of those things.

Throk felt a faint vibration from the concealed plate against his back. It lasted only a moment. The flyover and northbound transfer might be connected; neither the vibration nor the silence supplied a name or a motive.

The dragon continued north. It neither landed nor spoke.

Sev used the moment to finish the sightline he was copying and read the line partly hidden beneath a payment sheet. He did not try to steal half the table. When the keeper looked back, the same papers were in the same places.

Outside, frightened people began finding angry things to say about their fright.

Kai watched the shadow leave. He knew what had happened the last time the party had rushed toward a dragon. He also knew what killing that dragon had failed to explain.

"We need the lantern order," he said quietly.

The guard followed them out of the records tent. He had decided that an escort was easier than understanding what they were doing. Pat accepted this with such immediate gratitude that he became uncertain whether it had been his idea.

"Good. You can show us who issued the signals."

At the eastern stores yard, lanterns stood in a row on a plank. Each had a shutter that could cover the light. Picks and pry bars waited under canvas. The equipment keeper was counting rope, and started again whenever someone spoke.

"I need the issue instruction," Pat said.

"Then I need a reason."

She showed the survey note. "One long flash. They sent us to verify the route, but this doesn't say who gives it."

The keeper looked at the guard. The guard shrugged in the way of someone who would prefer the problem to become another person's problem.

The keeper pulled a folded sheet from beneath a weight.

Lantern. Two observers. Their station had to see both Wheel and the low approach. After the drums began, they were to watch the town's defenders. One long shuttered flash only when those defenders moved north. If they remained in place, hold the flash and send a runner. No independent advance.

Pat read it slowly enough to remember. Sev stood where he could see it too.

"Can we take a copy?" she asked.

"You can take that copy if you sign that you received it. I'm not being blamed when somebody says they weren't told."

Pat understood the opportunity. A receipt would leave a trace. Refusing it might leave a guard asking harder questions. She signed for the instruction as part of the delivery inquiry, using the cover they had already established. There would be evidence that someone had asked. There would not be a magically erased trail.

The keeper returned to the rope. The guard told them their questions were finished.

"They are," Pat said.

On the way back toward Mara, Kai fell into step beside her.

"They mean to go back to the mill."

"Yes."

"We could stop two of them here."

She glanced at the watch post, the bell, the tents beyond it. "And warn everyone else before we understand the rest."

He breathed out through his nose. "I dislike this place."

"You don't have to like it to leave it usefully."

Mara was beside Doss, arguing with a man about where the cart should be unloaded. When she saw the papers in Pat's hand, she stopped arguing. The five adventurers were no longer asking the questions of couriers.

She took them aside.

"How much have you understood?"

“Enough to know that tonight’s work is another attempt to get behind the arch,” Throk said.

“I told you where they issue the work,” Mara answered. “I did not promise you would like it.”

For a moment Mara looked as though she might deny everything, call for the guard, and let the camp sort them out. Then Doss shifted on his improvised seat and winced. Her eyes went to him.

Floyd did not use the young man's injury as a threat. He simply said that Doss needed proper care and that the survey assistant wanted to leave with them.

"You're collecting people now?" Mara asked.

"Only the ones who ask," Pat said.

What Mara offered was not an alliance. She would say the delivery dispute remained unresolved and that the visitors were taking an injured runner and an assistant toward town. She would not say the plate had been delivered. She would not conceal their departure indefinitely. If her employer asked, she would protect herself.

Throk accepted the terms for what they were. A narrow opening, made by a woman deciding which trouble she could survive.

At the gate, the clerk complained about changing his ledger. The escort complained about people wandering. Mara complained about the chalk collapse. Between the three complaints, the departure became a piece of ordinary camp inconvenience.

The party walked out with a copied map, three connected instructions, two people who wanted the road south, and the plate still in Throk's custody.

No bell rang.

Sev kept listening for it until the wind moved the camp sounds away.

They did not stop to congratulate themselves. They found a sheltered place off the road where the northern camp was out of sight, checked that nobody had followed, and spread the papers on a flat stone. Doss sat with his leg supported. The survey assistant crouched beside Floyd.

Sev placed three small stones on the copied map.

"Wheel. Spade. Lantern."

At first, the answer seemed simple. Wagons on the road. Workers near the mill. Observers on the ridge. Three groups coming toward Greenest. A small attack coordinated by light.

Kai pointed at the road. "We meet the wagons before they cross."

"Maybe," Pat said. "Read what they're carrying."

Throk looked at the tally again. Straw. Shields. Drums. No supplies to remain in the town. No equipment to break into it.

"They want to look heavier than they are."

Sev moved the Wheel marker south along the only route wide enough for the wagons. The orchard path narrowed at its footbridge. The drainage track broke into stones and shallows. Neither would take a loaded cart, or even an empty one without work that would defeat the purpose.

"Here," he said. "On the road, where everyone in the north of town can see them."

Floyd placed Spade on the eastern track. Boots, tools, and a way along the water. He traced the approach to the mill entrance with one thick finger.

“The plate did not get the first crew through the broken mechanism,” Floyd said. “This crew means to brace beside the arch and break through the masonry. They still want what is behind it. They do not need our plate to try.”

The assistant nodded. “I measured the outside approach. The breach order explains why they wanted the tools.”

Pat read the signal instruction again. Her eyes stopped on the condition.

"The flash doesn't follow the drums."

Kai frowned. "It says after the drums."

"After the drums, watch the defenders. The flash follows the defenders moving north."

The difference changed the map.

Sev lifted the Lantern stone to the ridge. Its observers could see the noisy wagons and the low eastern approach. From the lower ground they would lose one behind the folds of the land. The ridge connected the other two positions without anyone having to ride between them.

Throk leaned over the three markers.

"Wheel makes the town afraid. The town sends its people north. Lantern sees them leave. Spade goes below the mill through the gap in the defenses and makes its breach beside the arch."

"And if the town doesn't move?" Floyd asked.

Pat tapped the last sentence. "No flash. Send a runner. No independent advance."

There was a long silence while each of them imagined the same evening. Drums on the north road. People shouting from windows. Guards running toward the obvious threat. Eight pairs of boots moving quietly toward the place where they had worked so hard to pull one man out alive.

Sev looked almost offended by the elegance of it.

"They've drawn a map of what they expect people to do."

"Then we give them a disappointment," Kai said.

But understanding the plan did not choose their answer. They could seize the ridge. They could let the signal fail. They could prepare the mill. They could make a false movement and catch the group that followed it. Every option moved danger somewhere else.

Floyd looked south, toward the town they could not yet see.

"People need to know before the drums start."

That was the decision they could all agree on.

Sev rolled the map carefully. For once, the importance of possessing it had become less interesting than the importance of putting it on the right table.

The return to Greenest brought the imagined attack into uncomfortable focus.

From the northern road they could see the bridge, the approach beyond it, the roofs close enough together for a rumor to travel faster than a messenger. Eastward, the mill stood where the survey placed it. A person coming down the drainage track could remain partly hidden by the broken ground until frighteningly near the work entrance.

They also saw laundry. A child carrying an empty basket. A man repairing a shutter that still hung crooked after his repair. The ordinary persistence of a town that could not postpone every task until danger had passed.

Pat watched a pair of neighbors arguing about whether to reopen a stall. Tonight, those people might look north at the sound of drums and see exactly what somebody wanted them to see.

At the sanctuary, Floyd handed Doss over for care. He explained the injured leg and the pressure it had been under, keeping his account practical. The survey assistant waited close to the doorway, afraid that reaching town would turn them from witness into prisoner.

"Tell them what you measured," Floyd said. "Start there."

The worker nodded. It was a smaller task than explaining all the decisions that had brought them to the camp.

The party did not stop to resolve the pale stone, the broken channel, or every unanswered question in the town. Those matters had not disappeared. They had acquired another threat above them. There was still daylight, and they needed to use it.

Governor Nighthill listened in a room whose table had already been used for too many urgent purposes. Escobert the Red stood with his arms folded, his attention moving between the strangers and their papers.

Nighthill asked them to separate what they had witnessed, what the orders said and what they had inferred. This was not an arrest scene. No Greenest murder warrant existed. The mill rescue gave them something real to stand on, but it did not entitle them to unquestioned trust. If the threatened tavern guide had complained, his account would make the governor more guarded; the uncertain housekeeper memory would not be used as fact.

Sev opened the map.

Pat laid out the three instructions in the order they had understood them, rather than the order they had found them. Throk described the separate plate transfer and the dispatch about tonight’s work. Floyd explained the physical approach and the proposed masonry breach beside the arch. Kai described the watch post, the camp's readiness, and the dragon passing overhead.

Nighthill asked whether the dragon had spoken.

"No," Pat said.

Whether it had shown an intention to attack that evening.

"Not that we saw."

Whether they knew its name.

"No."

The governor nodded. Their refusal to fill the unknown spaces made the things they did know more useful.

Escobert called for someone familiar with the mill approach. The survey assistant repeated the measurements and pointed to the foot crossing. The account fitted the ground. He read the order again, his thumb resting beside the words about the defenders moving north.

"They expect us to run toward the noise."

"Would you?" Sev asked.

"Yesterday, with no better information? Probably."

There was no embarrassment in the answer. Escobert had enough work without pretending to have known things nobody had told him.

Nighthill looked around the table.

"You have shown us where they mean to put us. Now show us where we should stand. Escobert, tell them what we can actually spare."

The dwarf brought out wooden markers. Three small guard teams available for new assignments. Two volunteer work crews. One transport team with two carts. These were not all the people in Greenest. The keep still needed its ordinary watch. Patients still needed care. Families still needed food and somewhere to go. These were the resources that could be moved without pretending every other responsibility had vanished.

He placed them beside the map.

At first everybody wanted more of them.

Kai wanted the ridge watched and, if possible, taken. Sev wanted the main road observed without advertising a response. Floyd wanted the mill entrance protected and a safe route behind it. Pat wanted the people living near the mill moved before somebody decided to defend their houses from inside them. Throk wanted to know where the plate would be while all this happened.

Escobert waited until the suggestions stopped.

"That is five jobs. You have named three guard teams."

The room grew quieter.

Floyd moved one marker to the mill. "This one stays."

Kai set another near the ridge.

Pat touched it. "How do they get back if the camp sees them?"

"The same way they go up."

Sev shook his head. "That's what the people looking for them will watch first."

Kai looked down at the map again. The ridge had seemed like an answer when it was an enemy advantage. Now that he had imagined placing people on it, it had become a route, a withdrawal, a signal, and a distance from help.

He returned the marker to the edge of the table.

"Observe it first. Taking it makes a different promise."

Nighthill asked about evacuating the town.

Pat looked at the two cart markers. "Not everyone can leave at once. Not along the same road. And somebody has to be ready where they arrive."

Floyd pointed at the western footbridge. "Those carts cannot go over that. People on foot might, if the route is checked. Anyone who needs a cart needs another destination."

The map had ceased to be a collection of attractive possibilities. It was beginning to tell them no.

Throk understood that part. His father had protected roads through country where every agreement had enemies. A route was useful because someone could actually use it, not because a commander could point to it while speaking confidently.

"We aren't promising to get everyone out," he said. "We're deciding who needs to move, where they can move, and who will be there when they arrive."

Pat nodded. She proposed a limited relocation from the most exposed buildings near the mill, with shelter arranged in town. People who could walk would be guided. People who needed transport would use the carts on streets wide enough to take them. The orchard would remain a possible foot route, not a place to send a frightened crowd without checking it.

One volunteer crew could prepare the receiving space and help move people. The other could work with Floyd on a discreet obstacle near the mill approach, leaving access for inspection and a clear withdrawal route. No attempt to break into the ancient arch. No casual digging into damaged foundations. This was work above ground, directed at the threat they understood.

Escobert moved the two crew markers.

One guard team would hold the mill side. Another would show a controlled presence at the north approach without charging out after the wagons. The third would remain a reserve inside town, able to reinforce a real threat or help protect the movement of vulnerable residents.

"And if both places need them?" Nighthill asked.

They debated that longer than they had debated the initial positions.

Floyd wanted the reserve committed to the mill. Pat argued that the carts might need an escort. Kai warned that a feint could still hurt people if nobody respected it. Throk pointed out that keeping the reserve mobile meant accepting a delay wherever it went.

There was no arrangement that removed every risk.

They eventually set the priority: keep the withdrawal and care routes open, then reinforce confirmed contact at the mill. The north team would give ground toward its planned fallback if faced with more than it could hold. It would not pursue the drums beyond its orders.

Sev asked for observers with instructions to report what they actually saw. Not what they thought a shadow meant. Not what someone shouted from a roof. The ridge signal mattered, but the town would not make every decision depend on successfully watching one light.

Kai leaned over the map. "If the lantern flashes anyway, we don't assume our plan has failed. We find out what moved."

Escobert gave him a brief approving look.

The party chose to stay together near the mill at the opening of the operation, where Floyd's knowledge and their combined strength could matter most. Local observers would report from safer positions. Throk would keep the plate. Its custody would not be announced to the town or used to lure a dragon over people who had never agreed to be bait.

Pat asked who could change an order once the operation began.

It was a question that brought Nighthill fully into the conversation. He would authorize the plan. Escobert would coordinate the town's teams. Each team leader would know the fallback and would not need to wait for permission to avoid being surrounded. Reports and changes would go through named runners, repeated back before anyone moved.

"And the volunteers?" Floyd asked.

"They leave the work when fighting reaches it," Escobert said. "They are not a fourth guard team."

Floyd sat back. That mattered to him more than anyone at the table quite understood.

For a moment he could see another group of workers in another place, their hands black with stone dust, waiting for someone to tell them the next thing to try. He had spent years remembering the people he could not get out. Here were people he had not yet failed, with daylight still available and a map under his hands.

No golden voice spoke. No sign appeared in the wall.

He picked up a pencil and marked the route the work crew would use to leave.

Nighthill read the orders back. Each person corrected something. A position too vaguely described. A runner with two incompatible jobs. A cart route that needed to remain clear. The corrections made the plan smaller, less impressive in a speech, and more likely to survive contact with actual streets.

Finally Escobert set down the last marker.

"Then these are our positions. What is the signal to act, and what tells us to fall back?"

This time they had answers. Verified contact, specific reports, named responsibilities. A retreat triggered by a threatened escape route rather than by pride. A place for the wounded. A reason for each team to be where it stood.

Outside, the afternoon light had begun to soften. Somewhere north of town, two drums waited beneath a cover. Two observers would be expecting defenders to rush into view. Eight people with tools had instructions to watch for a long flash.

None of them knew everything that had happened around this table.

The messengers waited by the door. Nighthill looked at the five adventurers, then at Escobert, and gave his assent.

Pat released the corner of the map. Sev watched it curl slightly, then laid a weight on it. Kai straightened. Throk checked the cloth around the plate. Floyd brushed a trace of chalk from the route he had drawn.

The orders went out.

Beyond the windows, Greenest was still a town of real people doing ordinary things in the hours before evening. The map remained on the table, covered in promises that had not yet been tested.

That was where the story waited.
